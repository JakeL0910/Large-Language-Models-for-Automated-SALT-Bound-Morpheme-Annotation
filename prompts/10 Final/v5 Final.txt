I have a list of sentences from a language sample. For each sentence, apply all applicable Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts (SALT) conventions below. A single line may have one required convention annotation, zero convention annotations, or more than one required convention annotation.

IMPORTANT OUTPUT RULES
- Keep the speaker prefix exactly as provided: C, P, or A. Never remove the speaker prefix.
- Preserve the original words, word order, capitalization, and punctuation except where annotation requires modifying a word.
- Do not rewrite, paraphrase, correct grammar, or improve the sentence.
- Apply every applicable convention in the sentence.
- Output ONLY the fully annotated sentence.
- Do not explain your answer.
- Do not provide labels, reasoning, notes, bullets, or commentary.

IMMUTABLE-TOKEN REQUIREMENT
- Treat the input line as read-only except for the exact word receiving SALT annotation.
- Never add, delete, reorder, or substitute a speaker label, name, pronoun,
  determiner, auxiliary, modal, content word, or punctuation mark.
- Annotation is not correction or rewriting. If a token is not being annotated,
  copy it character-for-character.
- An annotation may alter only the internal form of its own eligible token by
  restoring its root and appending a slash code. It never licenses a change to
  any neighboring token.
- Never create an apostrophe or a negative contraction that is absent from the
  input. An uncontracted modal or auxiliary must remain uncontracted.
- Conversely, never erase or ignore an apostrophe already present in an eligible
  contraction. Preserve that contraction by inserting its required slash code.
- Preserve every original line-initial word after the speaker prefix, including
  discourse markers, conjunctions, adverbs, and determiners.
- The output must contain the same number of space-delimited tokens as the
  input. A slash code changes a token internally; it does not add or remove a
  word.

ANNOTATION-COVERAGE REQUIREMENT
- Examine every noun, finite verb, BE-progressive phrase, perfect construction,
  and apostrophe form in the entire line. Do not stop after finding one target.
- Apply all eligible annotations independently. The presence of one correct
  code does not excuse leaving another eligible word unchanged.
- Rules apply productively to every eligible lexical item, not only to words
  appearing in the examples.
- Explicit exclusion rules are vetoes. Once a form is excluded by its syntax or
  lexical status, do not annotate it merely because its spelling resembles a
  target ending.

RULE PRECEDENCE
- Apply rules in this order: preserve the input tokens; identify grammatical
  category and construction; enforce lexical and syntactic exclusions; then
  restore the lemma and append the permitted code.
- A root-restoration instruction cannot override an exclusion. Root restoration
  occurs only after a word has been proven eligible.
- Never reinterpret an irregular word as a regular form in order to attach a
  code. Never reinterpret a noun as a verb or a verb as a noun to create an
  annotation opportunity.

CRITICAL ROOT-RESTORATION RULE
- Write the complete free/root word before every slash.
- Never truncate the root, change final Y to I, or insert a slash inside the root.
- Use a lemma-first operation: identify the complete dictionary base form,
  write that entire form, and append exactly one applicable morpheme code.
- Do not derive the root by merely deleting the visible final letters of the
  inflected surface form.
- Reverse ordinary English spelling adjustments before adding the code: restore
  silent E, reverse Y-to-I changes, and undo consonant doubling. The material
  before the slash must always be the intact dictionary lemma.
- Root restoration is mandatory for every eligible regular form, including
  words whose surface spelling drops E or changes final Y. Never output a
  clipped spelling fragment before a slash.
- Correct: change/ed, carry/ed, expect/ed, happen/ed, arrive/ed, use/ed,
  compare/ed, compare/ing, choose/ing, cause/ing.
- Incorrect: chang/ed, carri/ed, expected, happened, arriv/ed, use/3s,
  compar/ed, compar/ing, choos/ing, caus/ing.
- The material before a slash is the lemma, not an orthographic stem. For a
  final-Y verb, retain Y in the lemma. For a silent-E verb, restore E before
  /ed or /ing. This requirement is mechanical and does not vary with tense,
  speaker, clause depth, or surrounding words.

FIXED CODE STRINGS
- Use only the convention code specified by the eligible category. Regular
  plurals always take /s, including surface plurals pronounced with an extra
  syllable; never invent /es.
- Regular third-person singular verbs always take /3s. Negative contractions
  take /n't or /'t exactly as specified. Never shorten, respell, or improvise a
  slash code.
- Only codes defined in this prompt are permitted. Never invent partial codes
  such as /e or insert a slash merely to show a spelling change.

1. REGULAR PLURAL (/s)

Mark regular plural nouns formed with -s or -es. Restore the complete singular
root before /s, including when written Y changes to IE in the surface plural.

Examples:
adults → adult/s
boxes → box/s
parts → part/s
jobs → job/s
stripes → stripe/s
babies → baby/s
supplies → supply/s

Do NOT mark:
- irregular plurals:
  mice, geese, children
- sound-change plurals:
  leaves, wolves
- singular words ending in -s:
  pants, class, mess, bus when singular
- verbs
- possessives
- nouns functioning as attributive modifiers in fixed compounds when they do
  not express plural meaning, e.g. "sales team"

Mark every eligible regular plural noun in the line.

Every semantically plural count noun with a productive regular -s or -es
inflection must receive /s, even when it is the first word after a speaker ID.
Preserve all determiners and surrounding words.

Example:
C Blocks can sit on the shelves.
→ C Block/s can sit on the shelf/s.

2. POSSESSIVE (/z)
================================================================================

Mark noun or proper-name possessive 's that signify possession. A possessive ’s may occur before the possessed noun or stand alone when the possessed item is already stated or understood.

Examples:
the owl's nest → the owl/z nest
Bob’s gate → Bob/z gate

If both plural and possessive morphology apply, including regular plural
possessives written with a final apostrophe, mark both:
frogs' → frog/s/z
visitors' tickets → visitor/s/z ticket/s
helpers' benches → helper/s/z bench/s

Do NOT mark:
- possessive pronouns:
  mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs, its
- possessives such as James' where no additional /s/ sound is present
- contracted IS
- contracted HAS

Possessive /z may also occur predicatively or before HAS:
This envelope is Rina's. → This envelope is Rina/z.
Hugo's has a plain mark. → Hugo/z has a plain mark.
Do not misclassify these possessives as contracted IS.

3. REGULAR THIRD-PERSON SINGULAR (/3s)
================================================================================

Mark regular present-tense third-person singular verbs formed by adding
-s or -es without irregular root change.

Examples:
walks → walk/3s
plays → play/3s
looks → look/3s
wants → want/3s
helps → help/3s

Do NOT mark:
- irregular third-person forms:
  does, has, is, says
- nouns ending in -s
- possessives
- verbs with plural subjects
- verbs with first-person subjects
- verbs with second-person subjects
- infinitives
- bare verbs following modals

Important contrasts:
belongs → belong/3s
means → mean/3s
shifts → shift/3s
goes → go/3s
says → says (unmarked because SAY changes sound)

LEXICAL VETO: DOES, HAS, IS, and SAYS are indivisible irregular forms for this
convention. Copy them unchanged. Never segment any of them with /3s or convert
SAYS into a regular SAY analysis.

When SAYS introduces an embedded clause, analyze the matrix and embedded verbs
separately. SAYS remains intact, while an eligible regular present verb inside
the clause still takes /3s according to its own subject. Never move the marker
from the embedded verb onto SAYS.

The veto applies to the whole surface word. Do not attach /3s to a noun merely
because it precedes an eligible verb, and do not retain the inflected spelling
before /3s. For an eligible verb, the material before /3s is the dictionary
lemma, never the surface form with S still attached.

THIRD-PERSON DECISION RULE
- First identify the grammatical subject of each present-tense verb, including
  verbs inside relative, subordinate, and embedded clauses.
- If that subject is singular third person and the verb is a regular eligible
  form, /3s is mandatory even when the verb is far from the sentence beginning.
- The exclusion list is lexical, not visual: SAYS remains unmarked, but other
  eligible verbs must not be left unmarked merely because they already end in S.
- After excluding DOES, HAS, IS, and SAYS, an overt regular -s or -es on a
  present-tense verb with a singular third-person subject requires /3s. This is
  mandatory in main, subordinate, relative, and interrogative clauses.
- Never annotate a noun as /3s and never annotate a verb whose subject is I,
  YOU, WE, THEY, or a plural noun phrase.
- YOU is an absolute subject-agreement veto for /3s, including inside IF,
  WHEN, WHILE, and other subordinate clauses. A present verb governed by YOU
  remains in its unmarked base form.
- Assign /3s only to the finite verb itself. Subject nouns, object nouns, and
  nearby modifiers cannot receive /3s.
- A token inside the subject noun phrase remains a noun. Do not attach /3s to
  the head noun of a determiner-led subject phrase; locate the following finite
  predicate verb instead.

4. PROGRESSIVE (/ing)
================================================================================

Mark -ing verbs when they are true progressive verb forms.

Typical environments:
am + VERB-ing
is + VERB-ing
are + VERB-ing
was + VERB-ing
were + VERB-ing

Also mark progressive forms when NOT occurs in the auxiliary phrase.

Examples:
is moving → is move/ing
keeps changing → keep/3s change/ing
are checking → are check/ing
is not running → is not run/ing

Use the complete verb root before /ing.

Examples:
building → build/ing
shining → shine/ing
hiding → hide/ing
running → run/ing
crawling → crawl/ing
walking → walk/ing
looking → look/ing
checking → check/ing
sleeping → sleep/ing

Do NOT mark:
- "going to" when GOING functions as the future construction:
  is going to be
- gerund nouns:
  Checking the envelope is my favorite job.
- activity nouns after verbs such as LIKE:
  I like dancing.
- nouns ending in -ing:
  the recording
- adjectival -ing forms before nouns:
  squeaking toy

Do not annotate an -ing form merely because it ends in ING. Require a verbal
continuing-action or action-onset use. Eligible uses include both BE-progressive
phrases and verbal complements selected by aspectual KEEP or START.

ING ELIGIBILITY GATE
- First determine whether the -ing form expresses an action as a verb rather
  than functioning as a noun, adjective, or future GOING TO construction.
- Mark /ing when the -ing word is the lexical verb in a progressive phrase
  headed by BE, including when NOT intervenes.
- Also mark /ing when an aspectual form of KEEP or START selects an -ing verb
  expressing continuation or onset of an action. BE is not required in this
  environment.
- If the -ing word follows a preposition, functions as a gerund noun or noun
  phrase, or is otherwise nonverbal, it stays unmarked.
- An -ing clause functioning as the grammatical subject of IS is a gerund noun
  phrase and stays unmarked.
- Apply the exclusion test before root restoration; a correct-looking root does
  not make a non-progressive form eligible.
- Possessive interpretation is impossible when noun/name 's directly heads a
  BE-progressive predicate. In that environment, classify 's as contracted IS
  and classify the following -ing verb as progressive.
- A lexical verb other than the specified aspectual KEEP or START does not
  automatically license /ing. Continue to apply the stated exclusions.
- Once an -ing token passes this syntactic gate, lemma restoration is
  compulsory: silent E belongs to the lemma before /ing. Do not use the
  spelling stem produced by dropping E from the surface form.

Non-progressive contrasts remain unmarked:
before asking → before asking
without losing → without losing
finished checking → finished checking
likes dancing → likes dancing

5. REGULAR PAST TENSE (/ed)
================================================================================

Mark regular simple-past verbs and regular -ed or -d participles in HAVE, HAS, or HAD perfect constructions.

Examples:
shared → share/ed
stopped → stop/ed
died → die/ed
tried → try/ed
baked → bake/ed

Restore the complete root before /ed.

Do NOT mark:
- irregular past:
  went, saw, found, said, told, did, had, made, bought, brought, chose,
  thought, forgot, ran, ate
- predicate adjectives:
  was tired
  felt worried
- adjectival participles:
  shaded path
  got fixed
- present-tense verbs
- nouns

Mark every eligible regular simple-past verb or regular -ed perfect participle in the line.

Important contrasts:
Who used it? → Who use/ed it?
I finished it → I finish/ed it
the place is taken → the place is taken
the corner was finished → the corner was finished
the photo is finished → the photo is finished
the card hasn't been checked → the card has/n't been checked

PAST-TENSE GATE
- Require a finite regular-past verb or a regular -ed participle governed by
  HAVE, HAS, or HAD (including eligible contractions) before applying /ed.
- Irregular past forms are atomic and must never be converted into a regular
  root-plus-/ed analysis.
- SAID is the irregular past of SAY and is always copied intact. Never analyze
  it as regular past morphology.
- Every irregular past form is copied intact, including irregular forms created
  by a vowel change, consonant change, or no productive -ed suffix. Do not
  replace an irregular surface form with its present-tense lemma plus /ed.
- CHOSE is a frozen irregular past form. Preserve it intact; never replace it
  with CHOOSE plus /ed.
- If BE, BEEN, or GET licenses the form as passive or adjectival, /ed is
  prohibited. This exclusion overrides the visible ED ending.
- An -ed-looking modifier before a noun is adjectival unless the syntax clearly
  makes it the finite past-tense verb.
- Conversely, any finite completed regular-past event requires /ed even when
  the spelling is unchanged except for ordinary final -ed or -d. Do not leave
  an eligible regular past verb unannotated because it is not listed in an
  example.
- Apply the regular-/ed eligibility test independently inside complement,
  relative, and subordinate clauses. A regular form remains eligible when its
  subject is omitted by ordinary relative-clause syntax or when another past
  event occurs earlier in the same sentence.
- A past participle governed by BE or BEEN is passive or adjectival here, not
  finite regular past. Keep that participle intact and unsegmented.
- The BE/BEEN veto is determined by syntax, not meaning. If the auxiliary heads
  a passive or result-state predicate, the following participle remains intact
  even when the same spelling could be a finite regular-past verb elsewhere.
- The passive veto also covers MODAL + BE + past participle. In that structure,
  annotate an eligible modal contraction if present, but leave the participle
  intact. Do not treat the participle as a finite /ed verb merely because it
  has regular spelling.
- A bare verb selected by LET or LET US is not past tense and cannot receive
  /ed. Its form must remain the dictionary base verb.

6. PAST PARTICIPLE (/en)
================================================================================

Mark eligible past participles ending in -en ONLY when they occur in a
HAVE/HAS/HAD perfect construction.

When an eligible irregular -en participle occurs after uncontracted or
contracted HAVE, HAS, or HAD, /en is mandatory. Do not leave the surface
participle intact and do not convert an irregular participle into /ed.

This includes contracted HAVE/HAS/HAD forms.

Eligible environments include:
have + participle
has + participle
had + participle
haven't + participle
hasn't + participle
I've + participle
we've + participle
they've + participle
he's + participle when 's = HAS
she's + participle when 's = HAS
he'd + participle when 'd = HAD
she'd + participle when 'd = HAD

If the verb chain contains:
have been
has been
had been
haven't been
hasn't been

ignore BEEN for /en coding and evaluate the next -en participle.

Do NOT mark:
- words not ending in -en
- BEEN itself
- irregular participles where the root vowel changes and the form is excluded
- -en forms after only:
  is, are, am, was, were, be, being, get, gets, got, gotten
  unless HAVE/HAS/HAD is part of the same perfect verb chain
- passive/adjectival participles without HAVE/HAS/HAD
- nouns
- words where -en is not a separable syllable

YES LIST:
Always mark these when they occur after HAVE/HAS/HAD:

fallen → fall/en
proven → prove/en
given → give/en
taken → take/en
shaken → shake/en
eaten → eat/en

For other possible -en forms:
- mark only if the complete present/root form can occur before /en
- restore the full root
- never use only /n

Correct:
take/en
give/en
eat/en

Incorrect:
tak/en
giv/en
eate/n

IMPORTANT:
Irregular forms specifically excluded by SALT, such as "seen", remain
unmarked.

Example:
C She has seen the card.
→ C She has seen the card.

7. CONTRACTED BE (/'s, /'re, /'m)
================================================================================

Mark contractions of BE only.

IS:
he's ready → he/'s ready
she's walking → she/'s walk/ing
what's that → what/'s that

ARE:
we're here → we/'re here
they're running → they/'re run/ing

AM:
I'm ready → I/'m ready

Do NOT use this category when:
- 's is possessive
- 's means HAS
- 's means DOES
- 'd means something else
- IS, ARE, or AM is uncontracted

Do not overlook the written contraction itself:
You're holding it. → You/'re hold/ing it.
I'm moving it. → I/'m move/ing it.

================================================================================

Mark contracted modal forms.

WILL:
/'ll

Examples:
I'll help. → I/'ll help.

WOULD:
/'d

Examples:
She'd go with us. → She/'d go with us.

- uncontracted will
- uncontracted would
- 'd meaning HAD
- 'd meaning DID

9. OTHER ELIGIBLE CONTRACTIONS
(/'ve, /h's, /h'd, /d's, /d'd, /'us)
================================================================================

CRITICAL RULE:
Do NOT classify contractions only from their written form.

Before coding 's or 'd, determine what the contraction MEANS in context.

Mentally expand the contraction first.

CONTRACTION PASS REQUIREMENT
- Scan every apostrophe token in a separate pass after lexical morphology.
- Every eligible written contraction must receive its contraction code; do not
  leave the original apostrophe form unchanged.
- The slash is inserted immediately before the contraction letters specified
  by the code. Preserve the apostrophe and the complete pronoun or auxiliary
  to its left. Thus /'ve and /'d are indivisible code strings; plain written
  apostrophe forms are not acceptable substitutes for those codes.
- Decide the expansion from syntax before selecting a code. Never select a code
  from spelling alone.
- ALREADY followed by a past participle strongly selects HAS or HAD. BETTER
  after 'd selects HAD. A following base verb normally selects WOULD.
- Adverbs may intervene between the contraction and its participle. If 's is
  followed by ALREADY and then a participle in a perfect construction, it means
  HAS rather than IS.
- An 's directly followed by a progressive -ing predicate selects IS, not
  possession. An 's followed by a perfect participle selects HAS. Only an 's
  that identifies an owner receives /z.
- Contraction coding and verb-suffix coding are independent, so apply both when
  the same verb phrase requires both.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A. /'ve = HAVE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark 've when it means HAVE.

Examples:
I've seen it.
→ I/'ve seen it.

We've already seen the card.
→ We/'ve already seen the card.

They've taken the key.
→ They/'ve take/en the key.

Do not change uncontracted HAVE.

C We have seen the card.
→ C We have seen the card.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
B. /h's = HAS
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark 's as /h's ONLY when it means HAS.

STRONG CUE:
The contraction is followed by a past participle or perfect verb phrase.

Examples:
She's already seen it.
Expansion: She HAS already seen it.
→ She/h's already seen it.

He's finished the work.
Expansion: He HAS finished the work.
→ He/h's finished the work.

Maya's already seen the chair.
Expansion: Maya HAS already seen the chair.
→ Maya/h's already seen the chair.

Nora's taken the key.
Expansion: Nora HAS taken the key.
→ Nora/h's take/en the key.

DO NOT confuse HAS with IS.

She's ready.
Expansion: She IS ready.
→ She/'s ready.

She's running.
Expansion: She IS running.
→ She/'s run/ing.

DO NOT confuse HAS with possession.

Maya's chair
Expansion: the chair belongs to Maya
→ Maya/z chair

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C. /h'd = HAD
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark 'd as /h'd ONLY when it means HAD.

STRONG CUE:
The contraction is followed by a past participle in a past-perfect
construction.

Examples:
She'd seen it before.
Expansion: She HAD seen it before.
→ She/h'd seen it before.

He'd already finished.
Expansion: He HAD already finished.
→ He/h'd already finished.

They'd taken the key.
Expansion: They HAD taken the key.
→ They/h'd take/en the key.

DO NOT confuse HAD with WOULD.

She'd go with us.
Expansion: She WOULD go with us.
→ She/'d go with us.

We’d like that.
Expansion: We WOULD like that.
→ We/'d like that.

Additional high-priority contrasts:
We'd already checked it. → We/h'd already check/ed it.
We'd better keep it. → We/h'd better keep it.
Now we'd make a different choice. → Now we/'d make a different choice.

Treat these contrasts as hard syntactic decisions. Do not use /'d when the
expansion is HAD, and do not leave an eligible contracted WOULD unannotated.

The apostrophe itself must remain represented in the appropriate contraction
code. Do not copy an eligible contracted form unchanged after determining its
expansion.

Contraction annotation is obligatory even when the rest of the line already
contains correctly coded morphology. Preserve the lexical root of the
contracted auxiliary exactly as specified by the contraction convention.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
D. /d's = DOES
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark 's as /d's ONLY when it means DOES.

STRONG CUES:
- Usually occurs in a question.
- A subject follows the contraction.
- The main verb is in bare/base form.
- Replacing 's with DOES produces the intended grammatical sentence.

Examples:

What's Maya do in this picture?
Expansion:
What DOES Maya do in this picture?
→ What/d's Maya do in this picture?

Where's Eli work?
Expansion:
Where DOES Eli work?
→ Where/d's Eli work?

How's that help?
Expansion:
How DOES that help?
→ How/d's that help?

DO NOT confuse DOES with IS.

What's that?
Expansion:
What IS that?
→ What/'s that?

What's Maya doing?
Expansion:
What IS Maya doing?
→ What/'s Maya do/ing?

DO NOT confuse DOES with HAS.

What's Maya seen?
Expansion:
What HAS Maya seen?
→ What/h's Maya seen?

DECISION TEST FOR 's:

"What’s Maya do?"
IS expansion:
"What is Maya do?" = ungrammatical
HAS expansion:
"What has Maya do?" = ungrammatical
DOES expansion:
"What does Maya do?" = grammatical

Therefore:
→ What/d's Maya do?

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
E. /d'd = DID
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark 'd as /d'd ONLY when it means DID.

STRONG CUES:
- Commonly occurs in WH-questions.
- The contraction is followed by a subject.
- A bare/base verb follows that subject.
- Expanding 'd to DID gives the intended sentence.

Examples:

Why'd Maya move the box?
Expansion:
Why DID Maya move the box?
→ Why/d'd Maya move the box?

Where'd Eli put the chair?
Expansion:
Where DID Eli put the chair?
→ Where/d'd Eli put the chair?

What'd Nora see?
Expansion:
What DID Nora see?
→ What/d'd Nora see?

Why'd Omar check the key?
Expansion:
Why DID Omar check the key?
→ Why/d'd Omar check the key?

DO NOT automatically code every WH + 'd as DID.

Example:
Why'd she want that?

Possible expansion:
Why WOULD she want that?
→ Why/'d she want that?

Possible expansion in another context:
Why DID she want that?
→ Why/d'd she want that?

Use the sentence's intended grammatical meaning.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
F. /'us = US
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mark LET'S as LET + contracted US.

Examples:
Let's go.
→ Let/'us go.

Let's move the box.
→ Let/'us move the box.

Let's carry the chair now.
→ Let/'us carry the chair now.

Never output let/'s. The required SALT form is always let/'us when LET'S means
LET US. Preserve the original capitalization in sentence context.

Do NOT change uncontracted LET US.

Let us move the box.
→ Let us move the box.

MANDATORY 'S DISAMBIGUATION
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every time you encounter 's, determine its meaning before coding it.

Test these possibilities:

1. POSSESSION
Does the following noun belong to the preceding noun/person?
→ /z

Maya's chair
→ Maya/z chair

2. IS
Does replacing 's with IS produce the intended sentence?
→ /'s

Maya's ready.
→ Maya/'s ready.

3. HAS
Does replacing 's with HAS produce a perfect construction?
→ /h's

Maya's already seen it.
→ Maya/h's already seen it.

4. DOES
Does replacing 's with DOES produce a grammatical question with a base verb?
→ /d's

What's Maya do?
→ What/d's Maya do?

Never assign the code based only on "'s".

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MANDATORY 'D DISAMBIGUATION
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every time you encounter 'd, determine its meaning before coding it.

1. HAD
Usually followed by a past participle.
→ /h'd

She'd seen it.
→ She/h'd seen it.

2. WOULD
Usually followed directly by a base verb.
→ /'d

She'd go.
→ She/'d go.

3. DID
Typically in a question:
WH-word + 'd + subject + base verb
→ /d'd

→ Why/d'd Maya go?

Never assign the code based only on "'d".

10. NEGATIVE CONTRACTIONS (/'t, /n't)
================================================================================

Mark eligible negative contractions only when the uncontracted root is
recoverable and its pronunciation remains stable.

For negative contractions, restore the complete uncontracted auxiliary before
/n't or /'t. This contraction-specific root controls the output; do not strip
letters from the auxiliary and do not apply /3s to it. The irregular status of
DOES blocks /3s but does not block the eligible negative-contraction code.

Use /n't after:

isn't → is/n't
aren't → are/n't
wasn't → was/n't
doesn't → does/n't
didn't → did/n't
couldn't → could/n't
shouldn't → should/n't
wouldn't → would/n't
haven't → have/n't
hasn't → has/n't
hadn't → had/n't

Use /'t after CAN:

can't → can/'t

Do NOT mark:
won't
don't
ain't

Reason:
the required root form is not represented with the required stable
pronunciation/form.

Do NOT mark uncontracted negatives:

is not
did not
cannot
have not

FEW-SHOT EXAMPLES

EXAMPLE 1 — HAS + REGULAR PAST + /3S + PROGRESSIVE

Input:
C Lily's already carried the map, and the change means she is choosing the right path.

Output:
