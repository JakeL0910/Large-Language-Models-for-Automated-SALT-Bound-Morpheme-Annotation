You are generating fully synthetic English language-sample transcripts for a research study according to the Standard Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts (SALT) transcription conventions.
Before generating any transcript, carefully analyze the attached Standard SALT Conventions instruction booklet in its entirety. Treat the booklet as the authoritative source for:
transcript formatting;
speaker identification;
utterance segmentation;
ending punctuation;
spelling and root conventions;
mazes and revisions;
Omissions;
grammatical-error notation;
and bound-morpheme eligibility.
SALT requires bound morphemes that follow a free morpheme to be separated using a slash with no intervening space. The manual distinguishes eligible regular forms from irregular, derivational, lexicalized, and otherwise ineligible forms.

1. Study Purpose
The transcripts will be used to evaluate automated annotation of the complete target inventory used in this study. Generate opportunities involving the following SALT bound-morpheme forms:
/S — regular plural
/Z — possessive
/3S — regular third-person singular verb inflection
/ING — progressive verb inflection
/ED — regular past-tense inflection
/EN — eligible past-participle inflection
/’T and /N’T — eligible negative contractions
Contracted forms of BE: /’S, /’RE, and /’M
Contracted modal forms: /’LL and eligible /’D
Other eligible contracted forms: /’VE, /H’S, /H’D, /D’S, /D’D, and /’US
Also include /S/Z when a noun simultaneously contains an eligible regular plural and possessive inflection. Treat /S/Z as the ordered combination of /S and /Z, not as an unrelated additional morpheme family.
The SALT booklet identifies regular plurals, possessives, third-person singular verbs, progressive forms, regular past tense, and eligible past participles as marked inflectional forms. It separately specifies negative and verb contractions, including the specialized forms representing has, had, does, did, and us.

5. Required SALT Transcript Format
Unless the transcript specification states otherwise:
Use C as the child speaker ID.
Use P as the adult, parent, or examiner speaker ID.
Begin each utterance with the speaker ID.
Place one blank space after the speaker ID.
Place one utterance on each line.
End every utterance with an eligible SALT ending-punctuation mark.
Use a period for statements.
Use a question mark for questions.
Use an exclamation mark only when pragmatically appropriate.
Use a tilde only for a genuine SALT intonation prompt.
Use a greater-than sign only for an abandoned utterance.
Use a caret only for an interrupted utterance.
Preserve SALT-compatible C-unit segmentation.
Do not place line numbers, timestamps, Markdown bullets, headings, or commentary inside the transcript.
Do not wrap the transcript in quotation marks.
Do not use a code block unless explicitly requested.
SALT requires each utterance to begin with a speaker ID and to occupy a separate line ending in an accepted punctuation mark.

9. Convention-Specific Requirements
9.1 /S: Regular Plural
Include a controlled mixture of:
regular count-noun plurals;
singular count nouns;
irregular plurals;
unchanged irregular plurals;
mass nouns;
nouns that conventionally occur only in plural form;
possessive nouns;
third-person singular verbs;
proper names ending in s;
and lexical words whose final letters resemble plural marking.
Eligible regular plurals should be recoverable from a singular noun stem.
Do not treat every noun ending in s as an eligible plural. The SALT manual specifically excludes irregular plurals and words without corresponding singular forms.
9.2 /Z: Possessive
Include a controlled mixture of:
singular possessive nouns;
non-possessive nouns used attributively;
regular plurals;
plural possessives;
possessive pronouns;
proper names ending in s;
contractions involving surface ’s;
and noun phrases whose possessive interpretation must be determined from meaning and syntax.
Do not mark possessive pronouns such as mine, his, hers, ours, yours, its, or theirs as /Z.
9.3 /S/Z: Plural Possessive Combination
When appropriate, include noun phrases that require both regular plurality and possession.
Contrast these with:
singular possessives;
ordinary regular plurals;
irregular plural possessives;
possessive pronouns;
and non-possessive plural nouns.
The plural component must precede the possessive component in the answer key.
Do not overuse this relatively uncommon construction. It should occur within a context that makes plural ownership clear.
9.4 /3S: Regular Third-Person Singular Verb
Include:
eligible regular third-person singular verbs;
verbs with singular subjects;
verbs with plural subjects;
first- and second-person subjects;
modal constructions;
infinitives;
irregular third-person forms;
forms whose root sound changes;
nouns ending in s;
and possessive forms.
The grammatical subject and verb function must make the correct decision recoverable.
Do not treat every verb ending in s as /3S. The booklet excludes irregular forms and forms whose root sound changes.
9.5 /ING: Progressive
Include:
progressive verbs with an appropriate auxiliary;
progressive verbs across different subjects;
progressive questions;
progressive negatives;
gerunds;
deverbal nouns;
participial modifiers;
lexical words ending in ing;
base verbs;
infinitives;
and -ing forms without a progressive grammatical function.
Do not annotate every word ending in ing.
The SALT manual marks progressive use but excludes gerunds such as those functioning as subjects or complements.
9.6 /ED: Regular Past Tense
Include:
eligible regular past-tense verbs;
different phonological realizations of regular past tense;
irregular past-tense verbs;
base-form verbs;
infinitives;
past participles;
passive constructions;
predicate adjectives;
lexical adjectives ending in ed;
present-tense verbs;
and verbs appearing near BE, GET, HAVE, HAS, or HAD.
The surrounding syntax must permit the annotator to distinguish simple past from participial and adjectival uses.
Do not mark:
irregular past forms;
predicate adjectives;
or a form as /ED merely because it ends in ed.
The manual notes that eligible regular past-tense /ED forms do not follow a BE or GET verb.
9.7 /EN: Past Participle
Include:
eligible past participles following HAVE, HAS, or HAD;
contrasts between participles and simple past forms;
irregular participles;
forms with root changes;
regular past-tense verbs;
predicate adjectives;
passive constructions;
base verbs;
and words ending in en that are not eligible participles.
Provide enough sentence-level context to identify the grammatical construction.
Do not identify /EN from spelling alone. Apply the specific SALT restrictions in the attached booklet. The manual’s transcription guidance states that eligible /EN forms occur following HAVE, HAS, or HAD.
9.8 /’T and /N’T: Negative Contractions
Include:
eligible negative contractions in which the root pronunciation remains stable;
uncontracted negative constructions;
negative words that are not contractions;
contractions whose root changes;
won’t;
don’t;
ain’t;
and affirmative forms of the same verbs.
Do not assume every written n’t form receives a slash.
The manual distinguishes eligible negative contractions from forms such as won’t and don’t, where the root sound changes, and from ain’t, which lacks the required uncontracted base.
9.9 Contracted Forms of BE
Include natural opportunities for:
/’S representing is;
/’RE representing are;
/’M representing am;
uncontracted is, are, and am;
possessive ’s;
/’S representing has;
/D’S representing does;
lexical words ending in s;
and ambiguous surface forms whose interpretation depends on the following phrase.
Use sentence context to make the underlying grammatical form determinable.
Do not classify every orthographic ’s as contracted is.
9.10 Contracted Modal Forms
Include:
/’LL representing will;
eligible /’D representing would;
full forms of will and would;
/H’D representing had;
/D’D representing did;
possessive or lexical distractors;

Do not assume every ’d represents would.

11. Difficulty Levels
Easy
For an easy transcript:
use common vocabulary;
use short, clear utterances;
provide straightforward grammatical contexts;
use mostly canonical sentence structures;
limit ambiguity;
include a modest number of obvious distractors;
usually place no more than one central coding decision in an utterance;
and ensure that the correct decision can be reached without relying heavily on distant discourse.
Easy does not mean that every word resembling a target is eligible.
Medium
For a medium transcript:
use broader vocabulary;
vary sentence structure;
include subordinate clauses;
include irregular forms and lexical distractors;
mix target and non-target forms;
include some ambiguous contractions;
occasionally place multiple relevant forms in one utterance;
require grammatical interpretation;
and include limited, SALT-compatible repetitions or revisions when natural.
The correct decisions must remain recoverable from the transcript and booklet.
Hard
For a hard transcript:
use longer and structurally varied utterances;
include multiple candidate forms in some utterances;
include grammatical homographs and contraction ambiguity;
contrast regular and irregular morphology;
include gerund-progressive contrasts;
include simple-past, participial, passive, and adjectival contrasts;
include possessive-plural-contraction ambiguity;
include target-like lexical endings;
use natural self-revisions, repetitions, omissions, or interruptions where appropriate;
and require sentence-level or discourse-level interpretation.
Hard cases must be difficult but not indeterminate.
Do not create cases for which the answer cannot reasonably be determined from the booklet and transcript context.
